I started weight training about 1.5 years ago. I started as I wanted to gain muscles and be stronger physically, which was not achievable by what I enjoyed doing (e.g. swimming, yoga). 

I had a strong start -- training about 3 times per week, but predictably, like all my previous attempts to attend gyms, it soon cooled off (I blame menstruation for killing the momentum). Yet it didn't die. My desire to become stronger always resurfaces, so I would restart training again. In total, I have attended about 35 sessions since I started.

I thought all I did was in vain as my weight hardly changes at all. That's until I bought a new body scale and found out that my bone mass has improved from below average to above average. My body fat has also reduced by 4%, and I have gained muscle. Better still, my metabolic age has also reduced by 3 years. I have to say I hardly feel any difference during the period, although the metrics seemed to tell a different story.

It suddenly occurred to me, that those are perhaps another example of compounding effects. By repeatedly making a small decision to train, I've achieved an effect size that's beyond my expectation. 

At my life stage, this is perhaps one way, or the only way, of making big changes in life. Why? Let me first define what is midlife crisis.

I define the midlife crisis as the unfulfilled state of living on by the force of inertia, after having made most of the big, largely irreversible decisions in one's life -- where to study, what subject to major, which career to choose, whom to marry, whether and when to have children, where to live, and when to buy a house.

I made most of the big bets of my life in the last 15 years, and they forever and dramatically changed my life trajectory. It's hard to imagine had I not decided to go to Barcelona to study public health (very impulsive decision after reading SanMao's books). Or had I not decided to go back ivory tower to do PhD (again, very impulsive decision, quitting my job one day and starting preparing documentation the next). Or had I not decided to message Xinxing when I was notified by Linkedin about her new job, which led to a hiking meet-up in Box Hill, where I met Leyi, my future husband.  

Now the decisions I'm left with are trivial and largely reversible. If I buy the wrong size/style of clothes, I can return it for free in 30 days. If I pick the fruit that's less tasty than I expect, I can just buy something else next time. I can pause Spotify anytime and switch tracks; I can pick up and put down books without committing to any at bookstores. Even when I teach Daniel something wrong, I can overwrite the teaching in a few days.  

But not all small decisions are equally small. Some disappear the moment they're made. What I have for lunch today hardly matters tomorrow. Others accumulate. One weight-training session is trivial; thirty-five apparently aren't.

Perhaps midlife crisis happens when we run out not only of major decisions, but of the sense that our decisions can still substantially change our future. When I was younger, life changed through a handful of big bets. Perhaps now it changes through hundreds of small ones.

Being stronger is just one of them. I have quite a few others, and before getting to them, let me book tomorrow's training session first!
